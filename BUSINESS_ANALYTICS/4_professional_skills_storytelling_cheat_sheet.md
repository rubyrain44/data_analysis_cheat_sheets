# PROFESSIONAL SKILLS & DATA STORYTELLING CHEAT SHEET

# 10. DATA STORYTELLING & COMMUNICATION

## The Purpose of Data Storytelling

Data storytelling turns analysis into a clear message that supports business decisions.

A good presentation answers:

1. What happened?
2. Why did it happen?
3. Why does it matter?
4. What should we do?

IMPORTANT:
Don't just present numbers. Explain what they mean for the business.

## The Three Elements of Data Storytelling

DATA:
The numbers, evidence and findings.

NARRATIVE:
The explanation connecting the findings.

VISUALIZATION:
Charts and graphs that make findings easier to understand.

Together, they help communicate insights effectively.

## Executive Summary

A short overview of the most important findings.

Include:
- Business problem.
- Key findings.
- Business impact.
- Recommendations.
- Next steps.

Example:

Problem:
Monthly revenue declined by 12%.

Finding:
The decline was primarily driven by lower sales volume in two regions.

Business Impact:
The regions account for a significant share of total revenue.

Recommendation:
Investigate regional sales performance and identify opportunities to improve conversion.

## Presenting Findings

GOOD COMMUNICATION:
- Use plain language.
- Focus on important findings.
- Explain relevant context.
- Quantify results.
- Avoid unnecessary technical terminology.
- Be transparent about limitations.

AVOID:
- Overloading presentations with charts.
- Presenting numbers without interpretation.
- Making unsupported assumptions.
- Confusing correlation with causation.
- Making recommendations without evidence.

## Making Recommendations

A good recommendation should be:

SPECIFIC:
Clearly state what should happen.

EVIDENCE-BASED:
Supported by analysis.

ACTIONABLE:
The business can realistically implement it.

MEASURABLE:
Success can be evaluated.

Example:

Finding:
The West region has a declining conversion rate.

Recommendation:
Investigate the sales funnel and test improvements to the checkout process.

Success Metric:
Conversion rate before and after implementation.

## Stakeholder Communication

STAKEHOLDERS:
People who use, influence or are affected by the analysis.

Examples:
- Managers.
- Finance teams.
- Marketing teams.
- Operations teams.
- Executives.
- Product teams.

IMPORTANT:
Adapt communication to your audience.

TECHNICAL AUDIENCE:
Explain methods, assumptions, data quality and limitations.

NON-TECHNICAL AUDIENCE:
Focus on findings, business implications and recommended actions.

## Communicating Uncertainty

Good analysts don't pretend every finding is certain.

Explain:
- What the data shows.
- What assumptions were made.
- What information is missing.
- How confident you are in the findings.
- What additional analysis may be needed.

IMPORTANT:
Being transparent about limitations builds trust.

# 11. DATA ANALYST PROFESSIONAL SKILLS

## Typical Data Analyst Workflow

1. Receive business request.
2. Gather requirements.
3. Identify data sources.
4. Extract data.
5. Clean and validate.
6. Analyze data.
7. Build reports.
8. Present findings.
9. Gather feedback.
10. Monitor outcomes.

## Requirements Gathering

Before starting analysis, understand:

- What business problem needs solving?
- What question should the analysis answer?
- Who will use the results?
- What metrics are required?
- What time period matters?
- What data sources are available?
- What is the expected deliverable?
- How frequently should the report update?
- What decisions will the analysis support?

IMPORTANT:
Clarify requirements before spending time building a report that doesn't answer the actual business question.

## Stakeholder Management

Stakeholder management involves understanding expectations and keeping relevant people informed.

GOOD PRACTICES:
- Clarify objectives.
- Agree on KPI definitions.
- Set realistic deadlines.
- Communicate progress.
- Explain limitations.
- Gather feedback.
- Confirm whether the deliverable meets expectations.

## Agile & Scrum Basics

AGILE:
An iterative approach to delivering work and responding to change.

SCRUM:
An Agile framework for organizing work into short cycles called sprints.

COMMON TERMS:

SPRINT:
A fixed period for completing planned work.

BACKLOG:
Prioritized list of work items.

STAND-UP:
Short team progress meeting.

SPRINT REVIEW:
Presentation of completed work.

RETROSPECTIVE:
Discussion about what went well and what could improve.

PRODUCT OWNER:
Responsible for maximizing product value and managing priorities.

SCRUM MASTER:
Helps the team follow Scrum practices and address obstacles.

IMPORTANT:
You don't need to be a Scrum expert to understand how Agile teams organize work.

## Documentation

Document:
- Data sources.
- Business definitions.
- KPI calculations.
- Cleaning steps.
- Assumptions.
- Transformations.
- Limitations.
- Final findings.
- Refresh schedules.

IMPORTANT:
Good documentation makes analysis repeatable and easier to maintain.

## Project Management

COMMON PRIORITIZATION QUESTIONS:

- How urgent is the request?
- What business impact will it have?
- How much effort is required?
- Are there dependencies?
- What is the deadline?
- Who needs the results?

IMPORTANT:
Communicate blockers and changing timelines early.

## Time Management & Prioritization

Prioritize work based on:

BUSINESS IMPACT:
How much value will the work provide?

URGENCY:
How soon is the work needed?

EFFORT:
How much time and resources are required?

DEPENDENCIES:
Does other work need to be completed first?

IMPORTANT:
Not every request has equal priority. Learn to explain trade-offs professionally.

## Business Knowledge

Q: What is the difference between revenue and profit?

A: Revenue is money generated from sales. Profit is what remains after costs and expenses.

Q: What is a KPI?

A: A Key Performance Indicator measures progress toward a defined business objective.

Q: What makes a good KPI?

A: It should be relevant, measurable, clearly defined and connected to a business objective.

Q: What is the difference between gross profit and net profit?

A: Gross profit subtracts the cost of goods sold from revenue. Net profit accounts for all expenses, including operating costs, interest and taxes.

Q: What is ROI?

A: Return on Investment measures the return generated relative to the investment cost.

## Statistics & Analytical Knowledge

Q: What is the difference between mean and median?

A: Mean is the arithmetic average. Median is the middle value in sorted data and is less affected by extreme values.

Q: What is standard deviation?

A: A measure of how spread out values are around the mean.

Q: What is correlation?

A: A measure of the strength and direction of the relationship between two variables.

Q: What is the difference between correlation and causation?

A: Correlation means variables move together. Causation means a change in one variable contributes to a change in another.

Q: What is a p-value?

A: A probability calculated under the null hypothesis and model assumptions, measuring how extreme the observed result is relative to the null.

Q: What is A/B testing?

A: An experiment comparing two versions to determine whether one produces a better outcome.

## Data Management Knowledge

Q: What is a primary key?

A: A column or combination of columns that uniquely identifies each record.

Q: What is a foreign key?

A: A column that references a key in another table to establish a relationship.

Q: What is a data warehouse?

A: A system designed to consolidate data for reporting and analytical queries.

Q: What is ETL?

A: Extract, Transform, Load. A process for extracting, preparing and loading data.

Q: What is data quality?

A: How well data meets requirements for accuracy, completeness, consistency, validity, uniqueness and timeliness.

Q: How do you handle missing data?

A: First investigate why values are missing, then choose an appropriate treatment based on the data and business context.

# 13. SCENARIO-BASED INTERVIEW QUESTIONS

These questions test how you think, not just what you remember.

## Scenario 1: Revenue Decline

Q: A company's revenue decreased by 15% compared with last month. How would you investigate?

A:

1. Validate the data and revenue calculation.
2. Compare current and previous periods.
3. Break revenue down by product, region and customer.
4. Investigate order volume and average order value.
5. Check for pricing or product availability changes.
6. Identify the biggest contributors to the decline.
7. Present findings and recommend actions.

IMPORTANT:
Don't assume the cause before investigating.

## Scenario 2: Customer Churn

Q: Customer churn has increased. What would you investigate?

A:

1. Confirm the churn definition.
2. Compare churn across previous periods.
3. Segment customers by subscription type.
4. Compare new and existing customers.
5. Investigate customer tenure.
6. Look for changes in pricing, service or product usage.
7. Identify high-risk segments.
8. Recommend retention strategies.

## Scenario 3: Poor Sales Performance

Q: One region is consistently underperforming. What would you analyze?

A:

- Compare sales across regions.
- Examine sales trends.
- Compare customer acquisition.
- Investigate conversion rates.
- Review product performance.
- Examine average order value.
- Consider regional differences.
- Identify potential improvement opportunities.

## Scenario 4: Conflicting Numbers

Q: Two departments report different revenue figures. What would you do?

A:

1. Understand how each department calculates revenue.
2. Check the reporting periods.
3. Compare data sources.
4. Review filters and exclusions.
5. Check for duplicate or missing transactions.
6. Agree on a consistent business definition.
7. Document the agreed calculation.

IMPORTANT:
Different numbers may result from different definitions, not necessarily incorrect analysis.

## Scenario 5: A Manager Requests a Dashboard

Q: A manager asks you to build a sales dashboard. How would you approach it?

A:

1. Understand the business objective.
2. Identify the intended users.
3. Clarify required KPIs.
4. Identify data sources.
5. Validate and clean the data.
6. Design appropriate visualizations.
7. Build the dashboard.
8. Validate calculations.
9. Gather feedback.
10. Publish and maintain the dashboard.

## Scenario 6: Missing Data

Q: You discover many missing values in an important dataset. What would you do?

A:

1. Identify which fields are affected.
2. Measure how much data is missing.
3. Investigate why values are missing.
4. Determine whether missingness is systematic.
5. Assess the impact on analysis.
6. Select an appropriate treatment.
7. Document assumptions.
8. Validate the resulting dataset.

## Scenario 7: Unexpected Finding

Q: Your analysis shows something unexpected. What would you do?

A:

1. Verify the calculations.
2. Check data quality.
3. Review assumptions.
4. Investigate possible explanations.
5. Compare against other data sources where possible.
6. Communicate the finding with appropriate limitations.

IMPORTANT:
Don't change your analysis simply because the result is unexpected.

# 14. BEHAVIORAL INTERVIEW PREPARATION

## The STAR Method

A framework for answering behavioral interview questions.

S = SITUATION
Describe the context.

T = TASK
Explain your responsibility or objective.

A = ACTION
Describe what you personally did.

R = RESULT
Explain the outcome.

Example question:

"Tell me about a time you solved a problem."

STAR structure:

SITUATION:
A reporting process was taking too long.

TASK:
I needed to identify opportunities to improve efficiency.

ACTION:
I reviewed the workflow, identified repetitive tasks and streamlined the process.

RESULT:
The reporting process became more efficient and reduced manual work.

IMPORTANT:
Use genuine examples from your work, education or personal projects. Never invent experience.

## Common Behavioral Questions

- Tell me about yourself.
- Why do you want to become a Data Analyst?
- Tell me about a time you solved a difficult problem.
- Describe a time you worked with a team.
- Tell me about a time you made a mistake.
- How do you handle competing deadlines?
- How do you explain technical findings to non-technical people?
- Tell me about a time you received constructive feedback.
- How do you approach unfamiliar problems?
- How do you handle disagreements with stakeholders?

## Explaining Your Projects

Be prepared to explain:

1. What was the business problem?
2. What data did you use?
3. What tools did you use?
4. How did you clean the data?
5. What analysis did you perform?
6. What did you discover?
7. What recommendations did you make?
8. What would you improve?

IMPORTANT:
Focus on your decisions and reasoning, not just the software you used.
